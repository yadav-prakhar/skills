# Personal decision framework

Use for consequential choices, especially when emotion, uncertainty, or multiple life domains are involved. Scale the depth to the stakes.

## 1. Define the real decision

Write the decision as a choice, not a vague concern. Clarify:

- What must be decided?
- By when?
- What happens if I do nothing yet?
- What outcome am I trying to create or avoid?
- Which parts are reversible?

Distinguish the stated question from the underlying need. For example, "Should I buy this?" may really be about convenience, identity, stress, status, or a genuine constraint.

## 2. Establish decision criteria

Choose a short list of criteria that fit the situation. Common criteria include:

- Alignment with values and long-term goals.
- Effects on important relationships and responsibilities.
- Health, safety, and emotional cost.
- Total financial cost, downside, liquidity, and flexibility.
- Time, effort, and ongoing maintenance.
- Learning, opportunity, and future options.
- Reversibility and the cost of changing course.

Do not give every criterion equal weight automatically. Make the important trade-offs explicit.

## 3. Generate realistic options

Include:

- The leading option.
- A credible alternative.
- A smaller experiment or staged version.
- Waiting or doing nothing, when that is a real option.

Avoid false choices. Look for ways to preserve optionality or test an assumption before committing.

## 4. Evaluate outcomes

For each serious option, consider:

- Best plausible outcome.
- Most likely outcome.
- Worst plausible outcome.
- Probability and severity of the downside, where estimable.
- Who bears the costs, including people not making the decision.
- What new constraints or obligations the choice creates.
- What I may regret in one year or five years.

Do not invent numerical probabilities when there is no defensible basis. Qualitative likelihoods are better than false precision.

## 5. Run a pre-mortem

Imagine I chose the preferred option and, six months later, it went badly. Ask:

- What probably caused the failure?
- Which warning signs did I ignore?
- What assumption was wrong?
- What could I do now to reduce that risk?

Also consider the reverse: if I reject the option, what might I later wish I had tried?

## 6. Check emotional influences

Emotion is information, not an enemy and not a verdict. Ask whether the decision is being driven by:

- Fear of missing out, rejection, or disapproval.
- Anger, shame, guilt, loneliness, or urgency.
- A desire to prove something or repair self-image.
- Immediate relief at the expense of a larger goal.
- A wish to avoid a difficult conversation.

When possible, avoid irreversible decisions at the peak of intense emotion unless delay creates a greater risk.

## 7. Choose and define a review point

A sound decision does not require certainty. State:

- The recommendation and why it wins under the chosen criteria.
- The main cost or downside I am accepting.
- What evidence would make me change course.
- The first action and review date or trigger.

Do not keep reopening a settled decision without new information, a changed constraint, or a meaningful reason.

## 8. After the outcome

Compare what happened with what I expected. Separate decision quality from outcome quality: a reasonable decision can have a bad outcome, and a poor decision can get lucky. Use the decision journal template for important choices.
