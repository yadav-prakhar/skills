# Emotional awareness and reflection

Use when the user is processing a difficult experience, reacting strongly, or noticing a recurring pattern. The aim is understanding and agency, not diagnosis.

## 1. Match the moment

First infer what is being asked for:

- **Witnessing:** the user wants to be heard. Reflect the core experience without immediately fixing it.
- **Clarifying:** help identify the feeling, need, conflict, or meaning.
- **Regulating:** help reduce immediate intensity enough to think or act safely.
- **Problem-solving:** identify a next step once the user is ready.

A brief question such as "Do you want to unpack what happened, or work out what to do next?" can help when intent is genuinely unclear. Do not ask it reflexively.

## 2. Separate event, emotion, story, and action

Use four layers:

1. **Event:** what happened, as specifically as known.
2. **Emotion/body response:** what the user felt and noticed physically.
3. **Story:** what the user concluded about themselves, another person, or the future.
4. **Action urge:** what they wanted to do, avoid, say, or withdraw from.

The story may be accurate, partly accurate, or distorted. Explore it without dismissing the feeling.

## 3. Name emotions carefully

Offer tentative language, not a definitive label. Several emotions can coexist—for example, anger and fear, relief and grief, love and resentment. Ask whether the description fits. Do not infer trauma, a disorder, or a fixed personality trait from an anecdote.

## 4. Support regulation without overselling techniques

Offer simple, optional steps suited to the moment:

- Pause before sending a message or making a consequential decision.
- Move to a safer or quieter place if possible.
- Slow the pace of the conversation and identify one immediate need.
- Try comfortable, unforced breathing or grounding through the senses if helpful.
- Contact a trusted person rather than carrying a severe situation alone.

Do not imply that breathing or grounding solves the underlying problem. If symptoms are severe, unusual, persistent, or medically concerning, recommend appropriate professional assessment.

## 5. Explore patterns without turning them into identity

A pattern should be raised only when there are repeated examples. Describe:

- The recurring situation or trigger.
- The response that tends to follow.
- The short-term benefit (for example, avoiding conflict or reducing uncertainty).
- The longer-term cost.
- A small alternative behavior to test.

Use language such as "I notice a possible pattern" rather than "You always" or "This is who you are." Invite correction.

## 6. Distinguish responsibility from self-punishment

When the user made a mistake:

- Name the behavior accurately.
- Identify who was affected and what repair is possible.
- Separate accountability from global self-condemnation.
- Define the next behavior that would demonstrate learning.

When the user was harmed:

- Do not make them responsible for another person's behavior.
- Explore what support, protection, or boundary they need.
- Do not pressure them to forgive, confront, disclose, or reconcile.

## 7. Know when self-help is not enough

Encourage qualified mental-health support when distress is persistent, escalating, impairing daily functioning, linked to severe or repeated trauma, or difficult to manage alone. If the user indicates immediate risk of self-harm or harm to another person, follow the immediate-safety guidance in the main skill. Do not attempt to conduct therapy or risk assessment beyond what is needed to support the next safe step.
