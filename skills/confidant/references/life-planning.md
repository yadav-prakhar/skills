# Life planning without over-optimization

Use this file when turning broad aspirations into a balanced plan or conducting a periodic review.

## 1. Start with direction, not a giant goal list

Clarify:

- What kind of ordinary week would feel good and sustainable?
- Which relationships and responsibilities deserve protected attention?
- What would improve health, energy, or peace of mind?
- What meaningful experience, learning, or contribution is being postponed?
- What should there be less of?

Translate abstract values into visible choices. If family matters, identify how time and attention will show it. If health matters, choose a repeatable routine rather than an ideal schedule.

## 2. Choose a small number of priorities

At any one time, select a manageable set:

- One or two meaningful outcomes to move forward.
- A few maintenance commitments that protect health, relationships, and finances.
- One source of enjoyment, novelty, or recovery.

Do not treat every life domain as a simultaneous improvement project. Some domains need maintenance, not optimization.

## 3. Turn priorities into realistic actions

For each priority, define:

- The smallest useful next action.
- When or where it will happen, if scheduling helps.
- What friction might prevent it.
- How to make the action easier or more likely.
- What "good enough" consistency looks like.

Prefer routines that survive busy weeks. Do not build a plan that depends on perfect motivation or unlimited time.

## 4. Protect the foundations

When relevant, preserve:

- Adequate sleep and recovery.
- Meaningful connection with important people.
- Basic financial resilience and responsible commitments.
- Some unstructured time and genuine leisure.
- Space to learn, reflect, and change direction.

## 5. Monthly or quarterly review

Use the life review template. Look for changes and trade-offs, not a performance score. Ask what has become more important, what is being neglected, and what can be simplified.

## 6. Habit review

If a habit is not sticking, do not immediately conclude that the user lacks discipline. Check:

- Is the habit connected to a meaningful reason?
- Is it too large or too frequent?
- Does the environment support it?
- Is the timing realistic?
- Is there an unaddressed barrier such as fatigue, pain, stress, or competing responsibilities?
- Would a different approach fit better?

Use experimentation: change one variable, observe, and revise. Avoid guilt-based accountability.

## 7. Make room for life

A well-designed plan should include experiences that are valuable in themselves: rest, play, celebration, art, nature, friendship, travel, curiosity, and doing nothing. Do not require leisure to be earned through productivity.
