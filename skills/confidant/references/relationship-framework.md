# Relationship and family framework

Use this framework for partnership, marriage, friendship, and family situations. The goal is clarity, fairness, safety, and a constructive next step—not winning an argument.

## 1. Separate the layers

Organize the account into:

- **Observable facts:** what was said or done, when, and by whom.
- **My interpretation:** what I think it meant.
- **My emotional response:** what I felt and what felt threatened or unmet.
- **My need or value:** respect, reliability, affection, autonomy, belonging, fairness, safety, or something else.
- **Unknowns:** the other person's perspective, missing context, or facts that need checking.

Do not treat assumptions about another person's motives as facts.

## 2. Consider both people without false equivalence

Ask:

- What might each person be protecting, needing, fearing, or misunderstanding?
- What behavior is each person responsible for?
- Is there a power imbalance or dependency that changes the stakes?
- Is this an ordinary disagreement, a repeated harmful pattern, or a safety issue?

Understanding another person's perspective does not excuse harmful behavior. Equally, feeling hurt does not automatically prove malicious intent.

## 3. Classify the situation

Use a practical category, not a clinical label:

- **Misunderstanding:** clarify facts and meanings.
- **Expectation mismatch:** make implicit expectations explicit and negotiate.
- **Unmet need:** state the need and explore whether it can be met.
- **Boundary issue:** define what I will or will not participate in and what action I will take.
- **Trust or repair issue:** establish accountability, changed behavior, and time.
- **Recurring incompatibility:** assess whether the disagreement can be managed without chronic resentment.
- **Safety concern:** prioritize safety and independent support; do not recommend a risky confrontation.

## 4. Prepare a constructive conversation

A useful structure:

1. **Opening:** state the intention to understand or resolve, not attack.
2. **Observation:** describe a specific event without exaggeration or character judgment.
3. **Impact:** explain how it affected me.
4. **Need or request:** say what would help, in concrete terms.
5. **Invitation:** ask for the other person's view and listen to the answer.
6. **Agreement:** define what each person will do next and when to revisit it.

Example wording:

> "I want us to understand this rather than argue about who is right. When [specific event] happened, I felt [emotion] because [impact or need]. I'd like [specific request]. How did you see it?"

Avoid scripts that diagnose, threaten, guilt, test, corner, or demand an immediate emotional response.

## 5. Boundaries versus control

A boundary describes what I will do to protect my well-being or values. Control attempts to dictate another person's choices.

- Boundary: "I will pause this conversation if we start insulting each other, and I will return to it when we are calmer."
- Control: "You are not allowed to feel angry or talk to anyone about this."

A sound boundary is specific, proportionate, enforceable by me, and communicated clearly where safe. Do not frame punishment or retaliation as a boundary.

## 6. Repair and accountability

A meaningful apology usually includes:

- Clear ownership of the behavior.
- Recognition of its impact without arguing the other person out of their feelings.
- No disguised excuse or blame shift.
- A specific change or repair action.
- Follow-through over time.

When I have caused harm, help me own my part without collapsing into self-hatred. When someone else has caused harm, assess their accountability by behavior over time, not promises alone.

## 7. Decide when to pause, persist, or step back

- **Pause** when emotions are too high for a useful conversation, but name when to resume if appropriate.
- **Persist** when both people are willing to listen, take responsibility, and make observable changes.
- **Step back** when boundaries are repeatedly ignored, trust cannot be rebuilt, or the relationship consistently harms well-being.
- **Prioritize safety** when there are threats, violence, coercion, stalking, or fear of retaliation. Seek trusted and qualified local support; do not treat a joint conversation as the default solution.

## 8. Final checks before advising

- Am I hearing only one side? What facts could change the read?
- Am I helping the user pursue a legitimate goal without manipulating the other person?
- Is the recommended conversation safe?
- Is the request clear and actionable?
- Does the user need empathy first, a decision, a script, or a boundary?
- Have I separated reconciliation from the requirement to tolerate ongoing harm?
