# Organizational intelligence

Use this reference when a situation depends on how an organization makes decisions, allocates resources, distributes credit, or exercises influence. Treat the map as a model to test, not a secret truth to uncover.

## 1. Formal structure and real decision flow

Identify:
- Who owns the decision and who has approval authority.
- Who provides recommendations or controls the information used.
- Who implements the decision and bears its consequences.
- Who can delay, redirect, or veto it in practice.
- Whether decision rights are explicit, shared, or ambiguous.

The person with the highest title is not always the only relevant decision-maker. Conversely, do not invent an informal power broker when no evidence points to one.

## 2. Stakeholder model

For each material stakeholder, capture:

| Dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| Role | What formal responsibility do they hold? |
| Interest | What outcome may matter to them? What evidence supports this? |
| Incentive | What are they measured, rewarded, or penalized for? |
| Influence | What access, credibility, expertise, or authority do they have? |
| Dependency | What do they need from others? Who depends on them? |
| Position | Supportive, opposed, undecided, or unknown? On what evidence? |
| Relationship | What is the current level of trust and working history? |
| Constraint | What limits their choices? |

Separate known facts from hypotheses. People can have several interests and may change position when constraints change.

## 3. Influence is multidimensional

Influence can come from:
- Formal decision rights.
- Expertise and scarce knowledge.
- Control of budget, staffing, access, or priorities.
- Trust and credibility with decision-makers.
- Ability to coordinate other teams.
- Ownership of important operational processes.
- Timing, information quality, and framing.
- Reputation for judgment and execution.

Do not reduce influence to popularity, proximity, or political cunning.

## 4. Organizational incentives

Look for tensions between:
- Local team outcomes and enterprise outcomes.
- Short-term delivery and long-term reliability.
- Visible wins and invisible maintenance.
- Individual performance measures and shared dependencies.
- Risk avoidance and experimentation.
- Ownership, accountability, and resource control.

When a behavior appears irrational, ask what incentive or constraint might make it locally rational. This is a hypothesis-generating tool, not an excuse for misconduct.

## 5. Build a practical map

A useful map should answer:
1. What decision or resource is at stake?
2. Who formally decides?
3. Who shapes the decision and how?
4. What does each material party plausibly care about?
5. Where are the dependencies and bottlenecks?
6. What relationship or evidence could change the outcome?
7. What remains unknown?

Keep the map small enough to act on. Update it when behavior or evidence changes; do not turn it into a fixed personality profile.

## 6. Validate the map

Useful evidence includes explicit decision criteria, prior decisions, meeting participation, resource allocation, written feedback, repeated behavior, and direct clarification. A single delayed reply or ambiguous tone is weak evidence of opposition. Ask respectful questions when possible rather than relying on speculation.
