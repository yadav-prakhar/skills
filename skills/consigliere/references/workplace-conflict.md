# Workplace conflict, credit, accountability, and escalation

Use when dealing with difficult colleagues, disagreement, unclear ownership, credit disputes, repeated blockers, misconduct concerns, or possible retaliation.

## 1. Classify the problem before responding

Possible categories:
- Misunderstanding or communication mismatch.
- Unclear roles, ownership, or decision rights.
- Priority or resource conflict.
- Performance or quality concern.
- Recognition or credit dispute.
- Trust breach or repeated unprofessional behavior.
- Discrimination, harassment, retaliation, safety, or other misconduct.

The category changes the right channel and level of formality. Do not label a normal disagreement as sabotage without evidence.

## 2. Create a factual chronology

Record dates, events, direct statements, decisions, deliverables, witnesses where appropriate, impact, and follow-up. Distinguish contemporaneous records from later recollection. Keep records in approved systems and follow applicable policies. Do not secretly exfiltrate restricted data or violate privacy rules.

## 3. Address ordinary conflict directly when safe

Use specific observations, impact, and a clear request. Avoid character judgments, mind-reading, public shaming, and motive accusations. Seek clarification before escalating interpretations as facts.

## 4. Protect contribution and credit

For shared work:
- Clarify ownership and roles early.
- Use collaborative artifacts with transparent contribution history.
- Send concise progress summaries to relevant stakeholders.
- Credit others accurately while making your own contribution visible.
- Correct material inaccuracies calmly and close to the source.

A practical correction:

> "To make sure the ownership is clear, I led [specific work], while [colleague/team] contributed [specific part]. The outcome was [result]. The next step I am taking is [next step]."

Avoid retaliatory credit-taking or a dramatic public takedown unless there is a genuine need to correct a consequential record in that forum.

## 5. Decide when to escalate

Escalation may be appropriate when:
- Direct clarification has failed or is unsafe.
- There is a repeated pattern with material impact.
- A policy, safety, compliance, discrimination, harassment, or legal concern exists.
- Decision rights require a higher-level owner.
- Delay creates significant harm or operational risk.

Before escalating, define the outcome sought, factual basis, relevant policy or process, appropriate channel, and plausible risks. Channels may include a manager, skip-level leader, HR, employee relations, ethics/compliance, union representative, ombuds function, regulator, or independent counsel depending on the issue and jurisdiction. No channel should be described as guaranteed neutral or risk-free.

## 6. Retaliation and safety concerns

Prioritize safety and qualified advice. Avoid unnecessary confrontation. Preserve a factual chronology and seek independent support where appropriate. Do not make unsupported allegations or retaliate. Legal protections and reporting options vary by location and employment status; verify current jurisdiction-specific rules.

## 7. Repair, containment, or exit

Choose based on behavior over time:
- Repair where accountability and changed behavior are credible.
- Contain where collaboration is necessary but trust is limited.
- Escalate where the problem requires formal intervention.
- Exit where the environment remains harmful or the role is no longer viable.

Do not confuse forgiveness with restored trust or staying with professionalism.
